# Example: Alternate Self Letter

> Below is a real-looking output for someone choosing between a startup job and staying at their current company.

## Option A: Join the Startup

### Timeline

**Month 1-3:** I walked into an office with 15 people and no onboarding. Day 3, I was asked to fix a production bug in code I'd never seen. I stayed until midnight. I went home wondering if I'd made a terrible mistake. Week 3, the CEO had a 1-on-1 with me and said "we need your experience, that's why we hired you" — it was the first time I felt wanted for my skills, not my resume.

**Month 4-12:** We raised the Series B in month 5. I got to sit in the board meeting. The equity terms discussion was terrifying — I realized "paper money" can disappear overnight. Month 8, we lost a key client. The CEO gathered everyone and said: "We're fine, but next quarter is make-or-break." I didn't sleep well for 3 weeks. Month 11, we landed an even bigger client. The relief was physical.

**Year 2-3:** I'm now the engineering lead, 3 levels up from where I started. My salary is still 15% below my old one, but my equity is worth something real now. I have a say in product decisions. I don't just build — I decide what gets built.

### My Letter to the Current You

*Hey. It's me, the version of you who took the startup.*

I'm writing this from my apartment on a Tuesday night. I just got off a call with a potential hire, and I'm eating cold leftover pasta because I forgot to have dinner. My coffee cup from this morning is still on my desk.

I'm tired. But here's what I need you to know.

The first 6 months were the hardest of my career. I questioned everything. I thought about quitting every week between month 2 and month 5. I cried in the bathroom once. Not because anything terrible happened — because I was so out of my depth and nobody was coming to save me.

But here's the thing I didn't expect: **being out of your depth forces you to swim.**

Month 7, I made a technical decision that the old me would have spent 2 weeks agonizing over. I had to make it in 2 hours. It was the right call. I didn't even realize I'd leveled up until a colleague said "how did you know to do that?"

I can't tell you this path is better. It's harder. The money is uncertain. There's no 401k match. But I can tell you this: **I know who I am now.** The old job told me what I was worth. This job showed me what I'm capable of.

If you take it: save up 6 months of expenses first. Negotiate a minimum equity guarantee. And on week 3, when you're staring at code you don't understand after midnight — that's not the moment to quit. That's the moment you start becoming someone new.

Good luck. I'll be here when you arrive.

## Option B: Stay for Promotion

### Timeline

**Month 1-6:** The promotion process was smoother than I expected. My manager had my back. The raise came through — 22%. I felt validated. I bought a nicer chair.

**Month 7-18:** The new role came with more meetings, less coding. I started delegating things I used to love doing. The work became about managing output instead of creating it. I was good at it, but I felt myself getting rusty.

**Year 2-3:** I'm now a director. The money is excellent. I have a team of 12. But I look at job postings for startups and feel a pull I can't explain. Not because I'm unhappy — because I know there's a version of me somewhere doing something harder. And I wonder if that version is more alive.

### My Letter to the Current You

*Hey. It's the version of you who stayed.*

I'm writing this from my home office. I just finished a performance review cycle and my team hit all their OKRs. I got a bonus. The mortgage is fine. Everything is fine.

And that's the problem.

I can't say I regret staying. I didn't risk anything. I didn't lose anything. But I also didn't find out if I could survive losing everything and still come out ahead.

You know that feeling when you're about to jump off a diving board? I never jumped. I stayed on the edge, looked at the water, and said "maybe next time."

There's a version of you who jumped. I don't know what happened to him. He might have belly-flopped. He might have nailed it. But at least he *knows*.

If you stay: make a plan. A real one. "I'll stay for 2 years, learn X, save Y, then take the leap." Otherwise you'll stay for 5. Then 10. Then you'll be me — comfortable, competent, and quietly wondering what the other version of you is doing right now.
